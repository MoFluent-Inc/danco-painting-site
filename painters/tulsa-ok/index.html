Housing stock

Tulsa's Older Neighborhoods

Much of Tulsa's most recognizable housing dates to the 1910s through the early 1930s oil boom, when Glenn Pool and Red Fork wealth pushed the city's population past 141,000 by 1930. Bungalow and Craftsman styles dominate the historic core.

In the Swan Lake Historic District, Bungalow and Craftsman homes make up 35 percent of contributing structures, concentrated in the north half, with Tudor Revival and Colonial Revival common in the south half. In Yorktown, Bungalow and Craftsman accounts for 69 percent of homes, and together with Tudor Revival makes up 90 percent of the district; nearly 95 percent of Yorktown's homes were built before 1931. Owen Park, platted around 1910, is mostly one-story Bungalow and Craftsman homes built for the city's professional middle class. White City, platted in 1926 on a former dairy farm, runs later: Tudor Revival and the related Minimal Traditional style dominate its 1926-to-1930s construction. Maple Ridge spans the widest range, from two-story brick and clapboard mansions built around 1912 to smaller brick bungalows and Spanish stucco homes through the early 1930s, across roughly 700 single-family residences.

Construction across these districts mixes brick and wood-frame clapboard. Real estate listings for homes in Maple Ridge routinely point to original crown molding, wide baseboards, and refinished original hardwood floors as selling points, typical of the intact millwork these homes were built with.

The paint job

Working With Original Wood and Trim

Exterior painting

Original wood siding, trim, soffits, and window casings in Tulsa's oldest neighborhoods are now 90 to 115 years old and need ongoing maintenance repainting rather than a one-time job. Tulsa's humid subtropical climate, hot summers, cold winters with regular snowfall, and roughly 40 inches of yearly rain concentrated in spring, is standard exterior-paint-failure weather for aging wood.

Interior painting

Original crown molding, baseboards, and built-ins in Tulsa's bungalows and Tudor Revival homes are frequently preserved selling features. An interior repaint in these homes has to protect that original millwork, not just recoat modern drywall trim.

Historic district rules

What a Certificate of Appropriateness Does and Doesn't Cover

The Tulsa Preservation Commission lists 14 residential National Register historic districts. Several also carry local HP (Historic Preservation) overlay zoning, the layer that actually triggers a permit review: Swan Lake, North Maple Ridge, Yorktown, White City, and Owen Park among others. In an HP-zoned district, any exterior alteration or repair, new construction, or demolition needs Tulsa Preservation Commission approval first, following the Commission's Unified Design Guidelines.

There's a useful exception for painters: applying paint color to non-masonry surfaces, wood siding and wood trim, is treated as ordinary maintenance and does not require a Certificate of Appropriateness. Work on masonry, or anything beyond a straight repaint such as a siding swap or a trim profile change, does require review. In practice, a like-for-like exterior repaint in a Tulsa HP district generally isn't held up by permit review, but a bigger change should go through Tulsa Preservation Commission staff first, at (918) 576-5687 or preservationcommission@cityoftulsa.org.

Tulsa Painting Questions

Does painting my house in a Tulsa historic district need a permit?

Usually not for a straight repaint. Painting non-masonry surfaces like wood siding and trim in an HP-zoned district counts as ordinary maintenance. Anything beyond a repaint, like a siding swap or painting masonry, should go through the Tulsa Preservation Commission first.
