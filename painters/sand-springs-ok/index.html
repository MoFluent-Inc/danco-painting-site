An Industrial Town, Built on Purpose

Oil magnate Charles Page purchased 160 acres in 1908 to build an orphanage. The townsite was platted in 1911 alongside an interurban railway, and the city incorporated in 1912 with about 400 residents. Page built the town's economy around industry deliberately, and by 1932 that base included glass factories, oil refineries, cotton gins, and textile mills. Sheffield Steel, later Armco Steel, opened in 1929 and was once the nation's largest castings plant, with five hundred employees. Kerr Glass Manufacturing and the Pierce Petroleum and Sinclair Oil refinery, which operated into 1948, were also major early employers. Today Webco Industries, a tube manufacturer, employs well over 1,500 people from a facility of more than 2 million square feet.

A Middle-Aged Housing Stock

Sand Springs grew steadily rather than in a boom, from 4,067 residents in 1920 to 19,873 by 2020, roughly 14% growth from 2000 to 2020 alone, a fraction of Jenks' or Bixby's pace over the same window. Building permits have trended down recently: 46 single-family permits in 2022, 32 in 2023, 30 in 2024, and 18 in 2025.

Point2Homes reports a median construction year around 1981 for Sand Springs housing, with roughly 13% of homes built before 1950. We treat that figure as a general indicator alongside the permit data above rather than an exact number. Either way, the picture is a real pre-1950 layer of homes old enough for original wood siding and trim, sitting under a much larger mid-to-late-20th-century core, older than Jenks' or Bixby's post-2000 stock, but nowhere near as old on average as Bristow's oil-boom-era core. Median home value was estimated at $222,327 in 2024, up sharply from $78,000 in 2000.

Prattwood Estates and the Old Towne District

Prattwood Estates, with multiple phases including Prattwood Estates II and IV, is home to roughly 1,105 people. The historic downtown core is the Old Towne District, centered on Broadway Avenue and Main Street. In 2006, the city adopted design standards for the district and ran a matching-funds program for private facade improvements, with work completed on buildings including the Station House Restaurant Building and the Hooper Building by April 2007, and a further downtown improvement project starting in summer 2024. As with Bristow's downtown, this applies to the commercial core, not to residential neighborhoods.

New Growth: The Trails

The city bought a 65-acre site near 51st Street and Highway 97, called The Trails, to build 125 single-family homes plus walking trails, about 10 minutes from downtown. Builders had previously passed on the site because of limestone deposits, so the city is developing it directly, with roughly a year of site work before it is ready to build on. City leaders have said the driving constraint is a housing shortage, not a surplus, despite successful downtown commercial growth.
