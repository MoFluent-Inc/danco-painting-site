Housing stock

A City Built After 1990

Owasso stood at just 3,491 residents in 1970, grew to 11,151 by 1990, then jumped to 18,502 by 2000 and 28,915 by 2010, a 65.9 percent increase from 1990 through that period. The 2020 Census recorded 38,352 residents, with estimates near 44,211 today, continuing the same growth curve.

The city's own history page traces an original 1904 town plat, three north-south streets and eight east-west streets, around present-day North Main Street, where City Hall still sits at 111 North Main Street. But that original townsite was small: Owasso had only 379 residents within town limits by 1907 statehood. Since more than 90 percent of the city's 2020 population growth happened after that small original townsite era, the overwhelming majority of Owasso's current housing footprint was built during the post-1990 subdivision boom.

Named subdivisions from this era are large, amenitized, developer-planned communities. Stone Canyon, built within the former Oxley Ranch nature preserve, covers roughly 3,000 acres, of which 650 acres are common-use area, centered on a 100-acre lake with a sandy beach, fishing pier, and walking trails. Bailey Ranch is built around a public golf course and includes several HOA-governed sub-sections sold as distinct subdivisions: Bailey Ranch Estates, the Fairways (phased sections including Fairways III, IV, and V), Honey Creek at Bailey Ranch (its own neighborhood guide cites roughly 4,998 residents), and Lakes of Bailey Ranch. Preston Lakes is a multi-phase subdivision, Preston Lakes, Preston Lakes II, and Park Place at Preston Lakes, centered on shared access to Centennial Park's duck pond, fishing, and walking trails.

Repaint timing

A First or Second Repaint Cycle

Because Owasso's population nearly tripled between 1990 and 2020, most of the city's housing stock is now 20 to 35 years old, old enough to be due for a first or second exterior coat since the original build, concentrated in named HOA subdivisions rather than scattered across a historic core. Owasso's climate runs hot summers (average July high near 93°F) and mild winters with rare snowfall, plus roughly 40 to 42 inches of annual rainfall concentrated in spring, wear-and-tear exposure applied to a much younger, more uniform building stock than an older city carries.

Approval process

HOA Approval, Not a City Permit

Owasso has no historic-district overlay comparable to Tulsa's HP zoning. The operative constraint here is private HOA architectural review, and the city's own government recognizes this directly: Owasso's Neighborhoods page states that homeowners associations "have the legal authority to enact and enforce maintenance and design standards above and beyond those required by City ordinance."

A concrete example: Bailey Ranch Estates HOA requires homeowners to submit an Architectural Request Form to its Architectural Review Committee for approval before making any change to the outside appearance of a home, including, presumably, paint color, with a stated 7-working-day review turnaround. The HOA explicitly warns that a City of Owasso permit does not guarantee HOA approval, since HOA requirements can differ from city code.

The city also runs the Owasso Strong Neighborhood Initiative (OSNI), which maintains a map of registered neighborhoods coded as HOA, Neighborhood Network (voluntary, no enforcement authority), or apartment complex. That means the level of exterior-color control genuinely varies subdivision to subdivision and should be checked per job rather than assumed citywide.

Owasso Painting Questions

Do I need HOA approval before repainting my Owasso home?

Often, yes, but it depends on the subdivision. Owasso's Strong Neighborhood Initiative map codes neighborhoods as HOA, voluntary Neighborhood Network, or apartment complex. Bailey Ranch Estates, for example, requires an Architectural Request Form before any exterior color change. Check your specific subdivision before you paint.
