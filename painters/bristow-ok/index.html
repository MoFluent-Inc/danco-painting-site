Old Bones and New Subdivisions

Bristow's population peaked at 6,619 in 1930, during an oil boom so intense that an estimated 31,000 people lived within a few miles of town at its height. The town never had a second building boom. Population settled back to around 4,600 by 1970 and has stayed close to that level since, standing at 4,248 at the 2020 census.

That history is visible in the housing stock. A large share of Bristow's core homes date to the 1910s through the 1930s oil-boom period, with bungalow, ranch, and shotgun-house types typical of the older housing. Original wood siding and older trim profiles are a normal part of an exterior job here, not an exception.

Bristow is not only an old-housing market, though. City-Data recorded 50 new residential buildings in 2025 and 28 in 2024, and NewHomeSource lists new-construction communities in the Bristow area from builders including Lennar, with homes priced from $184,000 to $604,900. A painter working here sees both substrates: original early-20th-century wood and brand-new drywall and trim.

A Downtown With Brick Streets

Bristow has more brick-paved streets than any other town in Oklahoma, a legacy of its early-20th-century prosperity. The Downtown Bristow Historic District, centered on Main Street and 4th Avenue, preserves early-20th-century commercial buildings such as the 1928 Bristow Motor Company Building and the 1929 Bristow Firestone Service Station. These are commercial and institutional landmarks, not a residential historic district, and Bristow's municipal code does not appear to include a separate residential color-approval process.

Bristow's older core and its newer subdivisions call for different work. On the older homes, that usually means exterior painting on original wood siding and trim. On newer builds, it is often interior painting for new drywall and trim, or cabinet painting and staining in a new kitchen.
