Tulsa, OK

Much of Tulsa's most recognizable housing dates to the 1910s to 1930s oil boom, bungalow and Tudor Revival neighborhoods like Swan Lake, Yorktown, and Maple Ridge. In the city's historic-preservation districts, painting wood siding and trim counts as ordinary maintenance and doesn't need a Certificate of Appropriateness; work on masonry or anything past a straight repaint does.

Broken Arrow, OK

Broken Arrow doesn't have one housing story. A small downtown core has early-1900s Craftsman bungalows, while the rest of the city is one of the fastest-growing in Oklahoma, adding new subdivision homes every year in HOA-governed communities like Forest Ridge and Battle Creek.

Owasso's population nearly tripled between 1990 and 2020, so most of its housing dates to that subdivision boom. Named communities like Bailey Ranch and Stone Canyon are HOA-governed; Bailey Ranch Estates, for example, asks for an Architectural Request Form before an exterior color change.

Worth mentioning at the estimate

HOA and Historic-District Rules

Several communities across the service area have their own rules about exterior color changes, separate from anything the city requires. Tulsa's historic-preservation districts review changes to masonry and anything beyond a straight repaint. HOA-governed subdivisions in Owasso and Jenks commonly ask for an architectural-review submission before a color change goes ahead. If your home sits inside a historic district or an HOA, mention it when you request your estimate so it's accounted for from the start.
