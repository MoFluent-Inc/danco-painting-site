Power Washing

Pressure washing and soft washing are not the same service, and using the wrong one on the wrong surface is the most common and most damaging mistake in this trade. Danco matches the method, chemical, and pressure to what is actually being cleaned.

Choosing the right method

Pressure washing vs. soft washing

Pressure washing

High mechanical force, commonly 1,300 to 3,100 PSI, appropriate for concrete, brick, and other durable, non-porous hard surfaces that can tolerate direct force.

Soft washing

Low pressure, commonly 150 to 300 PSI, up to 500 to 1,000 PSI for vinyl siding specifically, combined with a cleaning chemical that does the actual work. This is the correct method for painted siding, roof shingles, stucco, and other surfaces mechanical force can damage.

PSI is the stripping force at the point of contact; GPM, gallons per minute, is the flow rate that actually rinses debris and chemical residue away. A high-PSI, low-GPM setup can be more damaging without cleaning any faster. Professional-grade equipment generally runs 1 to 4 GPM paired with 2,000 to 3,000 PSI for general-purpose work.

How a house wash is sequenced

How Danco's soft wash works

  1. Pre-rinse and protect. Landscaping, outdoor fixtures, and anything sensitive to overspray or runoff get pre-wetted and protected.
  2. Apply the chemical solution. Applied at low pressure, working bottom-to-top or as the product directs, with dwell time allowed for the chemical to break down organic growth and lift dirt before rinsing.
  3. Rinse at low pressure, top-to-bottom. The nozzle stays at least 10 to 18 inches off the surface using a wide fan pattern, never a narrow jet, to avoid forcing water behind siding or etching the material.

Concrete and other hard surfaces get true pressure washing instead, where nozzle angle, distance, and a consistent wand pass pattern still matter, to avoid etching or visible zipper-mark streaking from an uneven pass.

What is actually doing the cleaning

The chemicals behind the wash

Sodium hypochlorite is the standard soft-wash chemical, at concentrations that vary by surface: roughly 1 to 2% for delicate surfaces like vinyl, paint, wood, and windows; 2 to 4% for stucco, concrete, brick, and gutters; 3 to 6% for asphalt or tile roofs and heavy growth. Surfactant is not optional in the mix, since sodium hypochlorite alone runs off a vertical or sloped surface too quickly to work; surfactant makes the solution cling long enough to actually penetrate porous material and crevices where organic growth hides.

Rust and hard-water mineral stains need an acid-based product instead, not sodium hypochlorite. Oxalic acid is the standard choice, applied to the pre-cleaned stain, given several minutes of dwell time, then rinsed thoroughly, because the staining mechanism is oxidized iron bonded into the pores, a different problem than organic growth.

A local, not generic, problem

Oklahoma red clay is not ordinary dirt

Oklahoma's red soil is high in iron oxide and fine clay that wedges into concrete and siding pores, especially after spring rains and windy days when it splashes and blows onto lower siding and driveways. Power washing alone typically cannot lift it because it is a mineral bond, not surface dirt. Effective removal is chemical plus mechanical: an acid-based cleaner formulated for red clay and iron staining, given dwell time, then washed off, the same principle used for rust removal.

The special case

Roofs need a different method entirely

A pressure washer should never be used on composition asphalt shingle roofs. The high-pressure stream dislodges the protective granules embedded in the shingle surface, and those granules provide the shingle's UV protection, so granule loss shortens the roof's effective service life. The correct method for roof algae or moss is a soft wash chemical treatment combined with gentle mechanical debris removal, a soft-bristle brush, leaf blower, or garden hose, never a pressure washer.

Doing it right

Technique and runoff compliance

Nozzle distance and angle matter more than force alone. Wide fan nozzles, 25 to 40 degrees, are the appropriate choice for general house and driveway work; narrow 0-degree and 15-degree pencil-jet nozzles are specifically the ones that damage siding and concrete when held too close.

Under the federal Clean Water Act, discharging pollutants, a category the EPA classifies broadly to include many cleaning agents, into waters of the United States without a permit is illegal, and property owners, not just the contractor, can bear responsibility. Common compliance practice for residential work is discharging to grass, gravel, or a sanitary cleanout rather than a storm drain, and using biodegradable detergents with no visible sheen in the runoff, a real regulatory exposure for any company that offers this service, not just an environmental nicety.

A common pairing

Power washing before an exterior repaint

Power washing is the first prep step for any exterior repaint, not an optional add-on. Once washed, the surface needs a genuine dry-out period, commonly 24 to 48 hours depending on substrate, sun exposure, and humidity, before scraping and painting proceed. Painting a surface that looks dry at the top but is still damp underneath is a leading cause of early paint peeling. See our exterior painting page for the full prep sequence.

Frequently asked

Power washing FAQ

What does a power washing job typically cost?

Typical industry pricing: a two-car driveway, roughly 400 to 600 square feet, runs about $100 to $300; a full house exterior wash on a 1,500 to 2,500 square foot home runs about $200 to $600; general residential pressure washing runs $150 to $500 depending on the surface. Most contractors also carry a minimum service charge to cover setup and drive time. These figures reflect industry-wide averages, not a set Danco rate.

Will pressure washing damage my siding or roof?

Depends entirely on method and surface. Vinyl, painted wood, and stucco should be soft washed; asphalt shingle roofs should never be pressure washed at all; concrete and brick tolerate genuine higher pressure when the technique, distance, nozzle angle, and consistent motion, is correct.

Why does my driveway still have stains after washing?

Likely a mineral stain, rust from irrigation or Oklahoma red clay iron oxide, rather than organic dirt. These bond into the concrete's pores and do not respond to water pressure or standard detergent; they need an acid-based product with dwell time.

Is it safe for my landscaping and pets?

Depends on the chemical and concentration. Biodegradable surfactants, controlled dilution, and proper rinse technique are standard for minimizing plant and pet exposure, and landscaping gets pre-wetted and rinsed before and after chemical application.

Do I need this before you paint my house?

Yes for any exterior repaint. It is the first prep step, not an optional add-on, and it needs a genuine dry-out period before scraping and painting can start.
