Cabinet Painting and Staining

Cabinet refinishing is a prep-dominated job. Danco spends most of the project on cleaning, deglossing, priming, and cure time, not on the topcoat itself, because that sequence is what determines whether the finish holds up.

Two different jobs

Painting and staining are not the same process

Painting cabinets is prep-heavy: disassembly, degreasing, deglossing or sanding, priming, and two topcoats, roughly six to eight steps depending on the cabinet's condition. Staining, when cabinets are bare wood or being refinished rather than painted, is shorter: a light sand, stain application, a sealer coat, and a topcoat, about three steps.

A common middle option is gel stain applied over cabinets that already have some finish on them. Gel stain sits on the surface instead of penetrating, so it does not require sanding down to raw wood, and it does not blotch on woods like pine or cherry the way a penetrating stain can. It is applied with a rag rather than sprayed, usually needs more than one coat to build color, and takes more care to keep even in carved or recessed door profiles, where it can pool.

How the job is sequenced

Danco's cabinet painting process

  1. Disassembly and labeling. Doors, drawer fronts, and hardware come off, each one numbered to its original opening. Factory hinge mortises and door reveals are rarely interchangeable, so putting a door back in the wrong spot creates a visible fit problem later.
  2. Degreasing. Kitchen cabinets accumulate cooking oil film even where the surface looks clean. A degreasing wash removes grease and grime before anything else happens.
  3. Deglossing and sanding. Factory cabinet finishes are hard and slick, and new paint will not mechanically bond to an intact glossy film. A liquid deglosser followed by a scuff-sand dulls the sheen enough for the next coat to key in. This is the step most responsible for whether paint holds for years or peels within months.
  4. Grain filling, oak only. Oak's open pores telegraph through paint unless they are filled first with a grain filler pushed into the pores and sanded back once dry. Closed-grain woods and MDF slab doors do not need this step.
  5. Priming. Primer choice depends on the substrate underneath, covered below. A standard wall primer will not bond to a slick factory finish or to laminate and thermofoil.
  6. Sanding between coats. A light scuff between primer and topcoat, and between topcoats, knocks down raised grain and dust nibs so the final film reads smooth.
  7. Topcoat. Doors and drawer fronts are usually pulled to a garage, shop, or spray area and sprayed for the smoothest, most even finish. Face frames and boxes that stay in the kitchen are typically brushed, rolled, or sprayed with masking.
  8. Reassembly after cure, not just dry-to-touch. Doors go back on only once the finish has firmed up enough to resist sticking where painted surfaces touch. This distinction, covered in the timeline section below, is where a rushed job most often goes wrong.

Primers and topcoats

Matching the product to the substrate

Primer and topcoat choice depends on what is under the paint, not on a single default product for every kitchen.

Primer

Zinsser B-I-N shellac primer

The standard choice when tannin bleed is a risk, such as oak, cherry, or knotty pine, or when heavy stain and odor blocking is needed. Touch-dry in roughly 45 minutes and bonds to a wide range of substrates, including glossy factory finishes.

Primer

Benjamin Moore Stix bonding primer

Chosen for adhesion to slick, non-porous surfaces like laminate and melamine, and it has some grain-filling ability on oak. Some jobs use B-I-N first for stain blocking, then Stix as a second coat before the topcoat.

Topcoat

Sherwin-Williams Emerald Urethane Trim Enamel

Touch-dry in about an hour and recoatable in about four hours at 77°F and 50% relative humidity, with full cure stated at 7 to 30 days. The short recoat window is why a primer coat and two topcoats can often land in a single working day per side under good conditions.

Topcoat

Benjamin Moore Advance

A waterborne alkyd, dry-to-touch around 6 hours, but the manufacturer's minimum recoat interval is 16 hours, meaningfully slower than the Emerald Urethane option above. Full hardness takes 7 to 30 days, and shelves or heavily used surfaces should stay out of service for 3 to 5 days minimum.

Shop-only topcoat

Conversion varnish

A catalyzed, professional-shop finish mixed immediately before spraying. Once fully cured it resists household chemicals, grease, and steam better than either waterborne option, but it requires a spray booth or proper ventilation and is not brushed on-site, which makes it realistic for doors pulled to a shop, not for in-place face frames sprayed in an occupied kitchen.

What to expect

Timeline: dry to the touch is not the same as fully cured

On-site labor for a professional crew commonly runs 3 to 5 days for a small kitchen (10 to 15 cabinet openings), 4 to 7 days for a medium kitchen, and 7 to 10 days for a large kitchen (20 or more openings), covering prep, priming, topcoats, and dry time between steps.

The kitchen is typically usable throughout the job, since doors come off and boxes and frames are painted with fast-drying, low-odor waterborne products, but the kitchen will be without doors for roughly a week during a standard job.

Paint is dry to the touch in hours, but it is not fully hardened for much longer. Across manufacturer data and painter practice, the guidance converges on light use being safe around 7 days, no heavy use or stacking dishes and pans directly on painted surfaces for 14 days, and full hardness by 21 to 30 days depending on the product and conditions. Water-based products are consistently slower to fully cure than oil-based or catalyzed products.

What goes wrong

Why cabinet paint fails early

  • Skipping deglossing or sanding on factory-finished cabinets. New paint cannot mechanically key into an intact glossy factory topcoat. This is the single most common root cause of chipping and peeling within the first one to two years.
  • Wrong or no bonding primer on thermofoil or laminate. Thermofoil is a heat-fused vinyl wrap over MDF, smooth and non-porous, and paint has nothing to grip without a true bonding primer. Standard wall primer on thermofoil peels or bubbles within months.
  • Reassembling before cure, causing blocking. Blocking is when two painted surfaces that press together, like a door against its frame, stick to each other and pull the film apart when opened, because the coating has hardened on the surface but is still soft underneath. It is more common with water-based enamels and is a direct consequence of rushing reassembly.
  • Moisture behind thermofoil or veneer. Usually from a dishwasher, sink leak, or steam near a range, causing the MDF substrate to swell and the surface to bubble or delaminate regardless of paint quality. Danco flags this before quoting rather than painting over it.
  • Skipping grain fill on oak. Leaves visible wood-grain texture showing through an otherwise smooth painted color. It is a cosmetic issue, not an adhesion issue, but it is the most common source of "the painter did a bad job" complaints even when the paint itself is holding fine.

Questions we hear

Cabinet painting and staining FAQ

How long until I can use my kitchen normally?

Cabinets are typically usable, opening and closing doors, light use, within days, but full hardness for heavy daily use such as stacking dishes or scrubbing takes 2 to 4 weeks depending on the product.

Can you paint over my stained cabinets without stripping them?

Yes, with the correct prep: degloss or sand, then a stain-blocking primer if tannins are a risk. A full strip to bare wood is only needed for certain stain refinishes, not for painting.

Will the wood grain show through?

On closed-grain woods like maple or MDF slab doors, generally no. On open-grain oak, yes, unless grain filler is used first. This is worth discussing before the job starts since it changes the prep scope.

Can you spray in place instead of pulling the doors?

Technically yes for the face frames, but doors and drawer fronts pulled to a controlled space get the smoothest, most even coverage because they can lie flat and be sprayed from a consistent angle.

Is painting cheaper than replacing my cabinets?

Nationally, cabinet painting and refinishing runs roughly $30 to $70 per linear foot, or about $3 to $10 per square foot, well under a full replacement. Per-unit pricing runs roughly $70 to $125 per door and $30 to $110 per drawer front. These are national market ranges, not an Oklahoma-specific quote, so a Danco estimate is what applies to your kitchen.

Can you replace my hinges and hardware while the doors are off?

Since doors are already disassembled for painting, many cabinet painters install new soft-close hinges or updated pulls at the reassembly stage, since the cabinets are already apart. Ask about this during the estimate.

Garage spray scheduling

Scheduling around Bristow's climate

Cabinet work happens inside a climate-controlled kitchen, so exterior heat and humidity swings matter less than on an exterior repaint. Bristow's relative humidity still runs roughly 66 to 80% year-round with no dry season, which slows recoat and cure times relative to a manufacturer's lab baseline of 77°F and 50% relative humidity.

When doors are pulled to an attached garage for spraying, that space is heat-exposed. Bristow's July average high runs around 91.6°F, with a recorded extreme of 114°F, and an Oklahoma summer garage regularly exceeds the general 90°F upper end for interior-grade application. An unheated winter garage can drop below the 50°F lower end, so garage spray scheduling has to work around both extremes rather than assuming any day works.
