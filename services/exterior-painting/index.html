Exterior Painting

A quality exterior repaint starts with inspection and a real dry-out period after washing, not with the paint color. Most premature peeling traces back to painting a surface that was not fully dry, not to the product used.

Step by step

Danco's exterior prep sequence

  1. Inspection and documentation. Walking the building and photographing rot, chalking, mildew, caulk failure, and prior peeling before any work starts catches scope surprises early.
  2. Power washing, then a mandatory dry-out. Pressure washing in the 2,500 to 3,000 PSI range removes dirt, chalking residue, and loose material, but the surface then has to dry, typically 24 to 48 hours, before scraping or painting. Coating over wet or damp siding is the number-one cause of premature peeling.
  3. Scraping. Loose or failing paint gets scraped to a stable edge, because new paint only bonds as well as the layer beneath it.
  4. Sanding. Feathering scraped edges avoids a visible ridge line, and light sanding of intact old paint roughens it enough for the new coat to key in.
  5. Wood repair. Rotted wood is cut out and replaced, or repaired with an epoxy system: a liquid consolidant first to harden remaining soft fibers, then a two-part epoxy filler packed in layers no deeper than about 3/8 inch. Skipping the consolidant is the most common reason a DIY-grade epoxy repair delaminates within a year.
  6. Caulking. Gaps at window and door perimeters, siding joints, and trim seams get sealed. Siliconized acrylic latex is the general-purpose exterior workhorse; polyurethane is used specifically at masonry and concrete joints, since it bonds into brick and concrete pores in a way acrylic latex does not, but it needs to be painted since bare polyurethane cracks under UV within roughly 5 to 7 years.
  7. Priming. Bare wood, repaired areas, and any surface stripped to bare material get primed so the topcoat bonds to a uniform, sealed surface instead of a patchwork of bare wood, old paint, and filler.
  8. Masking and topcoat. Typically two coats on new or bare substrate, one coat on sound existing paint being refreshed, product-dependent.

A wet edge matters throughout: working in sections small enough that the leading edge is still wet when the next section is brushed or rolled into it avoids visible lap marks where a partially dried area gets double-coated.

Product selection by material

Matching the coating to your siding

Wood, fiber cement, and trim

100% acrylic latex, such as Sherwin-Williams Duration Exterior, is the standard modern topcoat because acrylic resin stays flexible through temperature swings, while older oil-based topcoats cure harder and more brittle, more prone to cracking under repeated expansion and contraction. Duration is self-priming and mildew-resistant, with a touch time around 1 hour and a recoat time around 4 hours under favorable conditions, and a minimum application temperature of 50°F.

Vinyl siding

Color choice is a real technical constraint here, not just aesthetic. Vinyl expands with heat, and darker colors absorb more solar energy than the panel was engineered for, which can cause warping that does not reverse. Guidance is to stay no darker than the siding's original color unless using a paint line formulated for vinyl, such as Sherwin-Williams VinylSafe colors, or to look for a light reflective value above roughly 55 if a darker shade is wanted.

Fiber cement and engineered wood

James Hardie fiber cement and LP SmartSide engineered wood both take acrylic finishes well and handle dark colors better than vinyl since they do not share vinyl's heat-expansion failure mode. LP SmartSide benefits from field recoat cycles roughly every 7 to 10 years, up to 12 to 15 years for the factory ExpertFinish product.

Brick and masonry

Standard acrylic latex is not the default recommendation here, since it forms a surface film that can trap moisture inside the masonry, contributing to spalling and efflorescence, the white powdery mineral deposits from trapped moisture pushing salts to the surface. Mineral silicate paint and limewash are the technically correct systems because they chemically bond to the masonry and stay vapor-permeable.

When the work happens

Getting the timing right in Oklahoma

Most quality acrylic latex exterior paints need ambient air and surface temperature at or above 50°F, with 50 to 85°F cited as the range most manufacturers use for reliable film formation and cure. A surface above roughly 100°F risks the film skinning over before it bonds properly, which shows up as blistering.

The surface being painted should stay at least 5°F above the dew point at application time, and above the dew point for at least 48 hours afterward, since a temperature swing that evening can still condense on a not-yet-cured film.

Spring and fall are the seasons most often cited for exterior work, landing inside a moderate-temperature, lower-humidity band. Bristow summer highs average around 91.6°F in July, with a recorded extreme of 114°F, and humidity runs roughly 66 to 80% year-round, so summer work stays viable if scheduled around the hottest midday hours and the shaded side of the structure. Oklahoma's freeze-thaw swing, from summer highs on a dark wall to winter lows well below freezing on the same wall, is a primary driver of cracked caulk joints and lifted paint at trim seams, which is why flexible acrylic topcoats and properly matched caulk at every seam matter here, not as an upsell.

What goes wrong

Why exterior paint fails early

  • Painting over damp or wet substrate. The most cited single cause of premature peeling. Painting the same day as a wash, especially on a shaded or humid day, traps moisture under the new film.
  • Moisture intrusion from behind the wall, not the weather side. Leaky gutters, failed caulk, and rotted wood let water in from the building side; the wood swells, the film stretches until it can't anymore, and it peels in sheets rather than flaking in small chips. This is a signal to inspect flashing, gutters, and caulk before repainting.
  • Painting a hot surface in direct sun. Heat causes the film to skin over before it can bond and level, showing up as blistering and making lap marks harder to avoid. The fix is sequencing work to the shaded side of the house as the sun moves.
  • Sealing brick with a film-forming acrylic instead of a breathable mineral coating. Brick moves moisture through the wall assembly; a non-breathable film traps that moisture, pushing it toward the surface as efflorescence or, in freeze-thaw conditions, contributing to spalling.
  • A dark repaint on vinyl siding without a heat-formulated product. Can warp or buckle panels beyond what they were engineered for. This is a substrate failure, not a paint failure, and needs to be flagged before the color is locked in.

Common exterior questions

Exterior painting FAQ

What does an exterior repaint typically cost?

Across the industry, professional exterior repaints run roughly $1.50 to $5.00 per square foot of wall area, with most straightforward jobs landing around $2.50 to $3.50 per square foot; a typical 2,000 square foot home lands in the $3,500 to $9,000 range depending on siding material, height, and prep needed. These figures come from industry-wide data, not a Danco price for your project.

How long will the paint last?

Widely cited industry ranges run roughly 3 to 6 years for standard-grade paint, 7 to 10 years for premium 100% acrylic formulations, and 10 to 15 years for elastomeric coatings, though actual life depends on sun exposure, substrate condition, and prep quality.

Does my old paint need to be stripped?

Only failing paint needs to come off. Sound, intact old paint is cleaned, deglossed or sanded, and used as the bonding surface for the new coat. A full strip is reserved for widespread peeling, alligatoring, or incompatible old coatings.

Will you match my HOA-approved color?

This is a real constraint in many subdivisions and needs the approved palette confirmed before quoting, since some HOAs restrict both color and sheen. See the service-area notes below for what this looks like city by city.

How many coats do I need?

Depends on the substrate and product. Many self-priming acrylics are labeled for one coat over sound existing paint and two coats on bare or new substrate, but a dark-to-light or light-to-dark color change often needs two coats regardless of substrate condition.

Why do exterior quotes vary so much between painters?

Prep scope is the biggest driver. Two houses of the same square footage can price a factor of three apart depending on how much scraping, wood repair, and caulking the surface needs, not because of paint brand differences.

Local regulations to know

Exterior color changes across our service area

Tulsa, OK

Districts such as White City and Owen Park sit under historic-preservation zoning, but paint color on non-masonry surfaces like wood siding and trim is treated as ordinary maintenance and does not require a Certificate of Appropriateness. A straight repaint of 90-to-115-year-old wood siding is generally not blocked by that review.

Owasso, OK

Most Owasso housing sits in named HOA-governed subdivisions like Bailey Ranch and Stone Canyon, where an Architectural Review Committee, not a city preservation office, approves an exterior color change. One HOA in Owasso publishes a stated 7-working-day review turnaround.

Jenks, OK

Jenks is a new-construction, HOA-governed suburb where at least one named HOA requires Architectural Committee approval, submitted in writing, before any exterior paint color change. HOA approval requirements here are routine, not exceptional.

Broken Arrow, OK

Broken Arrow splits two ways: HOA-governed communities like Forest Ridge and Battle Creek have recorded architectural covenants, while Central Broken Arrow carries no mandatory HOA restriction at all, giving owners there full autonomy over exterior color.
