Epoxy Flooring

Epoxy flooring is a concrete-substrate job before it is a coating job. Grinding, degreasing, and moisture testing determine whether the floor holds up for years or fails within one, and inadequate prep is cited as the root cause in up to 80% of epoxy floor failures.

The step a DIY kit skips

Moisture testing, not just a calendar date

New concrete needs a minimum cure period, commonly cited as 28 days, before any resinous coating goes on, since that is roughly when concrete reaches most of its compressive strength. But 28 days is a floor, not a guarantee: depending on the mix, humidity, and temperature during cure, a slab is not always genuinely ready for 60 days even though it looks and feels dry at the surface.

Two ASTM-standard tests measure different things. The calcium chloride test (ASTM F1869) seals a dish under a plastic dome on the slab for 24 hours and measures moisture vapor emission by weight gain, but it only characterizes the surface layer. The relative humidity probe test (ASTM F2170) drills into the slab and reads internal humidity at 40% of slab depth after 24 hours, and is considered more reliable since it captures moisture deeper in the slab. Best practice runs both. A slab outside the acceptable range, commonly cited at or below 3 pounds per 1,000 square feet per 24 hours for the calcium chloride test, or 75 to 80% relative humidity for the probe test, needs a moisture-mitigating primer or should not be coated with a standard system.

Surface preparation

Grinding, degreasing, and joint treatment

  1. Mechanical grinding, not just acid etching. Diamond grinding exposes the concrete's pores directly and creates a genuine mechanical bond profile. Acid etching is a lower-cost alternative but is inconsistent and produces only a minimal surface profile by comparison.
  2. Crack and joint treatment before coating. Static, non-moving cracks get chased open and filled with a rigid two-part epoxy filler. Control and expansion joints need a semi-rigid or flexible filler instead, because those joints are engineered to keep moving with the slab. A rigid filler in a joint meant to move causes the crack pattern to telegraph through the finished coating.
  3. Degrease. Years of vehicle use soak oil and grease into the concrete's pores; surface cleaning alone does not remove residue driven in deep, and epoxy cannot bond through that contamination.
  4. Rinse and dry completely. Commonly 24 to 48 hours in dry conditions after etching or grinding, so the surface is bone dry, not just surface-dry, before coating.
  5. Primer. Rolled thin and even to seal the concrete's pores and improve topcoat adhesion.
  6. Base coat, broadcast media, then topcoat. The pigmented color layer goes down first; flake or quartz is broadcast into it while wet for a decorative or slip-resistant finish; a clear topcoat, often polyaspartic for UV stability, seals the whole system.

Epoxy, polyaspartic, or polyurea

Choosing a coating system

Epoxy

The traditional, most common residential product. Standard epoxy is not UV-stable and will yellow with sun exposure over time. Cure to full usability is commonly 3 to 7 days, and it is the coating most associated with hot tire pickup on thinner products.

Polyaspartic

Cures dramatically faster, commonly ready for foot traffic in 4 to 6 hours and vehicle traffic in about 24 hours, with the full job often finished in a single day. It is UV-stable, will not yellow, and has better abrasion resistance and heat tolerance than standard epoxy.

Polyurea

Cures extremely fast, often within minutes, which makes it useful as a tough base layer, but it is not naturally UV-stable, so it is typically paired with a polyaspartic topcoat that provides the UV protection.

Product grade matters as much as chemistry. Water-based epoxy runs roughly $2 to $5 per square foot while 100% solids epoxy runs roughly $8 to $15 per square foot; many box-store "epoxy" kits are actually thin, single-component water-based acrylic coatings, not true two-part epoxy chemistry, and their low solids content means they cannot withstand hot tire pickup regardless of prep quality. This is one of the most common points of customer confusion, and it is worth clarifying before comparing quotes.

What to expect

Cure schedule after application

Foot traffic is commonly safe at 24 hours; vehicle traffic needs 48 to 72 hours; full chemical cure and maximum abrasion resistance takes 5 to 7 days. Cooler temperatures extend every stage of this schedule by roughly 24 to 48 hours per stage. Driving on the floor before it is ready can cause tire marks, soft spots, or delamination that does not reverse on its own.

What goes wrong

Why an epoxy floor fails

  • Hydrostatic pressure from uncontrolled moisture vapor. Water in the ground moves upward as vapor; a coating sealed over untested moisture builds pressure underneath the film and pushes it up from below, causing bubbling or full-sheet delamination unrelated to surface bonding quality.
  • Hot tire pickup. Tire rubber can reach roughly 140°F on a hot day, enough to soften a weak coating film; as the tire cools, it grabs and pulls at the softened film. This is largely a product-grade issue: thin, low-solids products cannot withstand it regardless of prep.
  • Coating over oil- or grease-contaminated concrete. A garage floor can look clean at the surface while contaminated below, and that becomes the exact spot where the coating peels first.
  • Coating too-new concrete without moisture testing. Even past the commonly cited 28-day minimum, a slab can still be internally wet enough to cause failure while looking and feeling dry at the surface.
  • Rigid filler in a joint designed to move, or filler not ground flush. Both cause the underlying crack or joint pattern to telegraph visibly through the cured coating.

Humidity and cure speed

Scheduling around Oklahoma's climate

Concrete is porous, and absorbed moisture that freezes expands and pops the surface pores, causing spalling and pitting; Oklahoma's clay-rich soils compound this since soil moisture expansion under a slab during freezing can heave the slab itself. A garage floor showing active spalling, heaving, or fresh cracking is evaluated as a substrate issue before an epoxy system is quoted over it, not coated as-is.

Bristow's relative humidity runs roughly 66 to 80% year-round with no dry season, and epoxy or polyaspartic systems want humidity below 80% during application, ideally below 60%, so a muggy Oklahoma summer morning can sit right at the ceiling where a hazy film defect called blushing becomes a risk. In extreme heat, the chemical reaction can also accelerate enough to trap air bubbles or fail to level before it sets, which is why comparably hot climates schedule application for cooler morning or evening hours during peak summer rather than midday.

Before you book

Epoxy flooring FAQ

What does epoxy flooring typically cost?

Industry-wide, professional installation runs roughly $2 to $12 per square foot depending on system, with most residential garage jobs averaging $5 to $7 per square foot. A typical two-car garage, 400 to 500 square feet, professional job runs about $1,200 to $6,000, with a mid-range three-coat system with color flake commonly $2,000 to $3,500. These ranges reflect industry-wide data, not a fixed Danco price.

How long before I can park in my garage again?

Foot traffic is safe around 24 hours, vehicle traffic needs 48 to 72 hours minimum, and full cure and maximum resistance takes 5 to 7 days, longer in cooler weather. Polyaspartic systems compress this significantly, often allowing vehicle traffic within 24 hours.

Why did a DIY epoxy kit peel after a year?

Almost always inadequate surface prep, no grinding, no degreasing, no moisture test, combined with a thin, low-solids single-component product that is not true two-part epoxy chemistry.

Will it yellow over time?

Standard epoxy can yellow or amber with sun exposure over years; polyaspartic topcoats are UV-stable and resist yellowing, which matters for a garage with direct sun through windows or an often-open door.

Can you coat a floor with cracks in it?

Yes, but the crack treatment matters. Static cracks get ground and filled rigidly, while active control or expansion joints need flexible filler, or the pattern will show through the finished coating.

Is it slippery when wet?

Broadcast flake or quartz media, or a texture additive in the final coat, adds slip resistance; a smooth clear topcoat alone can be slick when wet, a real consideration for a garage that sees rain or snow tracked in.
